# Keepsake Preparedness Curriculum — Gerontology / Estate-Preparedness Educator Report

*Guide-layer concept session, July 2026. See 00-synthesis.md for the consolidated concept.*

*Grounded in the existing product: the belongings binder (photo + story + wish), the Emergency notes page with its six prompt chips, the Family roles (owner / collaborator / viewer / trusted-contact protocol), the memorandum export, and the app-wide truth rule ("never assert what isn't true"). The curriculum extends the same voice: warm, agency-first, no morbidity, no promise the product can't keep.*

---

## Part 1 — The Full Preparedness Map

**Framing sentence for the whole curriculum (shown once, at the start):**
> "Getting your affairs in order" sounds like one enormous job. It's actually seven small ones, and you've probably already done pieces of several. We'll show you the whole picture, then take it one 15-minute visit at a time. You decide the order, the pace, and every answer.

**Ratings key.** Emotional difficulty: 🕯 1 = pleasant · 2 = neutral chore · 3 = requires a decision · 4 = touches loss or family friction · 5 = mortality/capacity head-on. Time = total, chunked into 15-minute sessions.

**The universal per-module contract:** every module ends with (a) something *captured in Keepsake*, (b) a clear statement of *what still needs a professional* (never blurred), and (c) a completion moment the senior can feel.

---

### Module 1 · "The Stories in Your Things" — the belongings layer *(already built)*
**Learning objectives:** Understand that the story is the most losable asset in the house; experience the photo → story → wish loop; learn that a wish is not a will (and why that's fine for now).
**Tasks (3):** 1. Photograph and voice-record the story of one treasured item. 2. Add "who I'd like this to go to" for that item. 3. Add two more items from one room.
**Time:** 3 × 15 min. **Difficulty:** 🕯 1.
**Keepsake captures:** everything in this module. **Professional needed:** none — but the module plants the seed honestly: *"A wish here tells your family what you want. To make it binding, it goes in your will or a signed memorandum — Module 4 shows you how, when you're ready."*

### Module 2 · "How Your House Works" — home & practical
**Learning objectives:** Realize preparedness includes Tuesday-afternoon emergencies, not just estates; produce the note a neighbor or child could act on tonight.
**Tasks (5):** 1. Water/gas/power shut-offs and where the breaker box is (in words — the app already coaches "no key locations or codes"). 2. Who holds a spare key / which neighbor to call. 3. Pets: vet, food, medications, who takes them. 4. Medications & doctors list — where the current list lives (e.g., "on the refrigerator," the classic EMS location). 5. Recurring house needs (furnace filter, the clock that needs winding — link items from Module 1).
**Time:** 2 × 15 min. **Difficulty:** 🕯 1–2 (this is competence, not mortality — the easiest "hard-adjacent" module in the whole map, which is why it comes second).
**Keepsake captures:** all of it, as Emergency notes (extends the existing prompt chips). **Professional:** none.

### Module 3 · "Your People" — choosing who does what
**Learning objectives:** Distinguish the four hats in plain language; understand one person may wear several hats but doesn't have to; learn the selection criteria (proximity, temperament, time — *not* birth order, *not* "who'd be hurt otherwise").
**Plain-language distinctions (canonical copy):**
- **Executor** — *the person who carries out your will after you're gone.* Named *in* the will; has no power while you're alive. Think: project manager for your estate.
- **POA agent (power of attorney)** — *the person who can act for you while you're alive*, if you're ill or unavailable — pay bills, sign papers. Their power **ends** at death (that's when the executor's begins). "Durable" just means it keeps working if you become unable to decide.
- **Healthcare proxy / agent** — *the person who speaks to doctors for you* if you can't. A medical decision-maker, not a money one. Named in your advance directive or a separate healthcare form.
- **Trusted contact** — *the person who can see this binder* through Keepsake's careful, verified process — a practical helper, not a legal role. The gentlest hat; often the on-ramp to the others.
**Tasks (4):** 1. Read the four hats; jot who comes to mind for each (private draft — "you can change every one of these"). 2. Name your trusted contact in Keepsake (the only hat Keepsake itself operates). 3. Record who currently holds, or who you intend for, executor / POA / healthcare proxy — plus "not decided yet" as a first-class, judgment-free answer. 4. Note the honest conversation each choice requires ("has Sarah agreed to be executor? ☐ asked ☐ agreed").
**Time:** 2–3 × 15 min. **Difficulty:** 🕯 3–4 (choosing between children is the friction point; the curriculum defuses with "hats are jobs, not rankings — the child who lives closest often gets the healthcare hat, the organized one the executor hat, and love has nothing to do with it").
**Keepsake captures:** the trusted-contact designation (real), and a *record of intentions* for the legal roles. **Professional:** making executor/POA/proxy real requires the documents in Modules 4–5 — the module says so explicitly.

### Module 4 · "Your Important Papers" — vital documents *(full sample content in Part 4)*
**Learning objectives:** Name the six core documents and what each does in one sentence; audit which exist; record where each lives and who can reach it; know which need a professional vs. a free state form.
**Documents covered:** will, durable POA, advance directive (living will + healthcare proxy), deed, insurance policies (life/home/auto/LTC), vehicle & other titles; supporting cast: birth/marriage certificates, military discharge (DD-214), Social Security card.
**Tasks (5):** see Part 4. **Time:** 3–4 × 15 min. **Difficulty:** 🕯 3 (the audit itself is a checklist; the "I don't have a will" discovery is the tender moment — met with "most people don't yet; now you know, and knowing is the whole assignment today").
**Keepsake captures:** the document *inventory* — exists?, where it is, who prepared it, who can access it. **Professional:** drafting the will and POA (attorney, or reputable online service for simple estates — stated with the tradeoff); advance directives are usually **free state forms** requiring witnesses/notary, not an attorney; deed changes always an attorney/title company. Keepsake never drafts, stores no originals, and says both plainly.

### Module 5 · "Your Health Wishes" — the advance-directive deep dive
**Learning objectives:** Understand an advance directive as *instructions you write while well, for a day you may never have*; separate the two halves (what I want / who speaks for me); learn it's free, revocable, and about **your voice being heard** — the strongest agency-framing in the curriculum.
**Tasks (4):** 1. Read the two-halves explainer. 2. Get your state's free form (Keepsake links the state health-department/AARP version — informational only). 3. Talk it through with your chosen proxy *before* signing — the conversation is the medicine; the form is the receipt. 4. Sign per your state's rules; record in Keepsake where it lives and who has copies (proxy + doctor should each have one — a copy in a fireproof box no one can open at 2 a.m. helps nobody).
**Time:** 3 × 15 min, deliberately spread across a week. **Difficulty:** 🕯 5 — the summit of the curriculum; everything before it builds the confidence to face it. Never assigned in the first two weeks; never adjacent to Module 8.
**Keepsake captures:** completion status, locations, proxy identity, and — a signature Keepsake move — an optional *voice note to your family about why you chose what you chose* (the story layer applied to the hardest document). **Professional:** witnessing/notarization per state; POLST only via a clinician.

### Module 6 · "Money, Accounts & Passwords" — financial/digital
**Learning objectives:** Understand that unfindable accounts become lost money (states hold billions unclaimed); learn beneficiary designations (POD/TOD) override the will — the single most surprising fact in the curriculum; set legacy contacts on the big platforms; know what a safe-deposit box does and doesn't do.
**Tasks (6):** 1. List every institution (bank, brokerage, pension, insurer) — *names only, never account numbers or balances in the note* (safety rule, consistent with the app's existing coaching). 2. Check/update beneficiary designations ("a form at the bank, five minutes, free, and it beats the will — worth a phone call this week"). 3. Password plan: where the list or manager master key is, described safely ("Sarah knows the phrase"), never the passwords themselves. 4. Legacy contacts: Apple, Google, Facebook (Keepsake provides walk-through cards). 5. Subscriptions & autopays list (what would keep charging; what would get shut off, like the electric bill). 6. Safe-deposit box: where, who's a co-signer — plus the trap in plain words: *"if only you can open it, it may be sealed exactly when your family needs it; your will should generally not live there alone."*
**Time:** 4 × 15 min. **Difficulty:** 🕯 2–3 (tedium more than grief; scam-safety framing keeps it motivating and connects to the existing Before You Sell shield).
**Keepsake captures:** the institution list, designation-check status, legacy-contact checklist, subscription list, box location/access. **Professional:** the designations themselves (each institution), digital-asset language in the POA/will (attorney — RUFADAA gets its one-line definition: "the law that decides who may access your online accounts; naming someone in writing is what makes it work").

### Module 7 · "The Family Conversation" *(taught, not hoped for)*
**Learning objectives:** Learn that telling people your decisions *now* is the #1 preventer of later fights; get scripts for announcing (not negotiating) decisions; learn the fairness toolkit.
**Tasks (4):** 1. Choose your format (one call, a dinner, a letter, or "let the binder talk" — share the story-first Family Summary, values hidden, which the product already supports). 2. Rehearse the core script: *"I've been putting my wishes in order — not because anything's wrong, but because I decided it's my job to make this easy for you. Here's what I've decided, and here's why."* Decisions are announced with reasons; input may be invited on unassigned items — the "Not yet decided" list becomes the agenda. 3. The fairness toolkit: story-value vs. dollar-value distinction; taking turns choosing; "equal isn't identical"; the one-question rule for children ("is there one thing that matters to you? — asking beats guessing"). 4. Hold it, then record what was said and any changed wishes (audit trail already exists).
**Time:** 2 × 15 min prep + the conversation itself. **Difficulty:** 🕯 4 — highest *interpersonal* risk; the curriculum's whole warm-first sequence exists so the senior arrives here holding a finished artifact instead of an awkward opener.
**Keepsake captures:** the summary that anchors the talk, conversation-held status, updated wishes. **Professional:** an elder mediator, if there's an already-burning conflict — named honestly as an option.

### Module 8 · "For Your Family: The First Days" *(audience: the adult child)*
**Learning objectives (child-facing):** Know the first-48-hours sequence; know the first-week calls; know what can wait (nearly everything financial); know where everything is *because Modules 2–6 wrote it down*.
**Content — first 48 hours:** 1. Pronouncement (expected at home: hospice or doctor; unexpected: 911). 2. Notify the shortlist, one person per branch to fan out. 3. Funeral home / anatomical wishes — this is where Mom's recorded preferences pay off. 4. Secure the house & pets — Module 2's note *is* the checklist. 5. Order **10–12 death certificates** via the funeral home (the number nobody knows they'll need). **First two weeks:** Social Security (funeral homes usually notify; survivor benefits need a call), the attorney holding the will, employer/pension, insurers, banks (with certificates), forward the mail. **What can wait:** the house, the belongings, most accounts. Rule in bold: **almost nothing must be signed in the first month — anyone who says otherwise is selling something** (the scam-shield ethos, extended to bereavement).
**Tasks (3, done by the child *now*):** read it; confirm you can reach the binder (trusted-contact verification path); fill your parent's gaps back into their task list (gently — through them, not around them).
**Time:** 2 × 15 min. **Difficulty:** 🕯 3 for the child; **the senior never has to read this module** — a deliberate design mercy, and honest, since it's not their job.
**Keepsake captures:** the whole reference lives in the binder and prints with the summary. **Professional:** probate attorney post-death; the module says when ("if there's a will, real estate, or any disagreement — before signing anything").

## Part 2 — Sequencing & Pedagogy for 65–85 (and their 45–60 children)

**1. The warmth gradient is the curriculum.** Canonical order 1→8 runs 🕯1 → 🕯5 → 🕯4 on purpose: stories build fluency with the app *and* with the subject of legacy before any document is named. Rule: **never two 🕯4+ modules in consecutive sessions**, and the first three sessions of any learner's journey must include at least one story capture — the emotional deposit that funds the later withdrawals.

**2. The 15-minute session is the atomic unit.** Each session = one card: *what we'll do, why it matters, what "done" looks like*. One decision or one artifact per session, never both heavy. Every session ends with a named completion moment — seniors abandon curricula that feel infinite; a visible "6 of 7 rooms of your life are lit" progress metaphor (rooms, not percent — matching the app's room model) beats a checklist of 40 items.

**3. Plain-language rules (hard requirements).** No term of art without a one-line definition *at every first use per module* (don't assume retention across sessions — assume interruption). Definitions follow the pattern *"X — the person/paper that does Y"*. Reading level ≤ grade 7. No acronym stands alone (RUFADAA, POLST, POD get glossed inline). Numbers get anchors ("10–12 death certificates — banks and insurers each want an original"). And the app's truth rule extends to teaching: **Keepsake explains and records; it never advises, drafts, or promises a legal outcome** — every module states its professional boundary in the module, not in a footer.

**4. Agency over mortality, always.** Frame is competence and authorship: "you decide," "your instructions," "make it easy for them," "so your family always knows what to do." Banned framings: "before it's too late," "when you're gone," "end-of-life planning" as a header. The advance-directive module is titled "Your Health Wishes," not "End-of-Life Care." Every hard module opens by restating revocability: *"Everything here can be changed later. Writing it down doesn't lock it in — it just means your voice is in the room."*

**5. When and how the adult child comes in.** Three-beat protocol: **(i) Momentum first** — the senior does Modules 1–2 alone or with light help; inviting the child before the senior has authored anything flips the dynamic to surveillance. **(ii) Invite at Module 3** — naming the trusted contact *is* the invitation, and it arrives framed as the senior's decision ("Margaret chose you"). **(iii) Parallel track** — the child gets their own three-lesson strand: *How to help without taking over* (ask about stories, never balances; add memories — the product's existing collaborator verbs), *What to do if you spot a gap* (route it as a question to the parent, not a fix), and Module 8. The child never sees the parent's incomplete hard modules as red badges — only "3 areas Mom is still working on," count without content.

## Part 3 — Trigger-Event Variants (one curriculum, four doors)

Onboarding asks one soft question — *"What brings you to Keepsake today?"* — and re-sequences. Same modules, different order, different first sentence.

**A. The downsizing move** ("we're sorting the house").
Sequence: **1 (intensive, room-by-room) → 7 (early!) → 6 → 4 → 2-at-new-home → 3 → 5**. The move forces belonging decisions anyway, so the story layer rides the sorting energy — every keep/give/sell decision becomes a capture prompt ("before this leaves the house, save its story — 90 seconds"). Module 7 moves up because distribution is happening *now*, not hypothetically; the fairness toolkit prevents the driveway argument. Module 6 rises because movers + open houses = paper exposure (safety framing). Deadline pedagogy: sessions keyed to the move date; the Before You Sell scam shield is featured (estate-sale predators are real). Tone: momentum, not grief — "the move is doing half the work for you."

**B. Recent widowhood.**
Sequence: **8-survivor edition (immediately, for the household) → 4 → 3 → 6 → …pause… → 1 → 5 → 7**. Reality: the survivor is *forced* into paperwork — certificates, accounts, retitling — so the curriculum meets the fire instead of offering stories to someone drowning in forms. Module 3 is urgent for a non-obvious reason taught explicitly: *the person who just died probably held most of your hats* (executor, proxy, POA) — every role must be re-chosen. Module 1 arrives only after the administrative wave, reframed as memorial: capturing Robert's watch story is grief work with a product-shaped container, and the existing family-memories feature lets children grieve alongside. Module 5 is delayed at least 8–12 weeks (a bereaved person confronting their own directive too soon is a known drop-off cliff). Pacing: sessions shrink to 10 minutes; "stop here today" is always a button; tone acknowledges without wallowing ("one thing at a time is the only way anyone has ever done this").

**C. The new diagnosis.**
Sequence: **5 → 3 → 4 → 6 → 1-voice-first → 2 → 7**, compressed. The organizing fact, stated with care and honesty: **legal documents require capacity, so the paperwork window is now, while every choice is unquestionably yours** — urgency framed as maximum agency, not decline. Health wishes and POA jump to the front; the physician conversation (and POLST, where applicable) is flagged as clinician territory. Module 1 becomes **voice-first and prioritized while speaking is easy**: "your voice telling these stories is the part no one can ever recreate" — the recorded-audio heirloom becomes the emotional payoff that makes the legal sprint bearable. The adult child is invited immediately (skip the momentum beat — this is the documented exception), with an expanded helper track. Tone rule: the word "still" is banned ("while you still can" → "while it's easy").

**D. "The kids finally asked."**
Sequence: canonical **1→8**, but the *child* enters first via the existing gift flow. The child's pre-work (before Mom sees anything): the three helper lessons plus a fourth — *How to open the topic without opening a wound*: lead with the story ask, not the money ask ("Mom, I realized I don't know the story of the clock" outperforms "Mom, do you have a will?" every time); one topic per visit; your role is scribe, not executor-in-waiting. The senior's experience is then the standard warm sequence with a co-pilot — and the curriculum explicitly hands the senior the wheel in session one ("Sarah set this up, but every answer in it is yours, and you can change any of them"). Guardrail: if the child races ahead requesting document info before the senior finishes Module 3, the child track interjects: "Ask her about a story first. The papers go faster after."

## Part 4 — Sample Module (complete): "Your Important Papers"

### Intro copy (Keepsake voice, first screen)

> **Your important papers**
> Somewhere in this house — a fireproof box, a desk drawer, a folder from 1987 — are a handful of papers that answer big questions for your family. Most people have some of them. Almost nobody has all of them, and almost nobody's family knows where they are. That's all we're fixing here.
>
> You won't write any legal documents today, and Keepsake never will — that's a job for a professional, and we'll tell you exactly which kind when one is needed. Your job is simpler and just as important: **know what you have, know what's missing, and write down where things are** so the people you love never have to search.
>
> This takes three or four short visits. We'll start with the easiest question in the whole binder: where do your papers live right now?

### Plain-language definitions (shown as cards; each ends with *who prepares it* and *where it should live*)

- **Will** — the paper that says who receives what you own, and names the person in charge of making it happen (your **executor**). Without one, your state's default rules decide instead of you. *Prepared by:* an attorney (best for most), or a reputable online service for very simple situations. *Lives:* the original with your attorney or in a fireproof box someone else can open — your family needs to know which.
- **Durable power of attorney (POA)** — the paper that lets someone you choose handle money and paperwork *for* you while you're alive, if illness or a hospital stay gets in the way. "Durable" means it keeps working even if you can't direct it. It ends at death — that's when the will takes over. *Prepared by:* an attorney; usually notarized. *Lives:* your agent needs to be able to produce it — a copy locked away from them defeats the purpose.
- **Advance directive** — your written health wishes for a day you can't speak for yourself, plus the name of the person who speaks for you (your **healthcare proxy**). It's not about giving up anything — it's about your voice being heard, exactly as you meant it. *Prepared by:* usually a **free form from your state** — witnesses or a notary, no attorney required. *Lives:* copies with your proxy and your doctor; the original somewhere reachable.
- **Deed** — the paper proving who owns your home. Your family will need it; changing whose name is on it is never do-it-yourself. *Prepared/changed by:* an attorney or title company. *Lives:* fireproof box; the county keeps the official record, so a lost deed is recoverable — knowing that saves a panic.
- **Insurance policies** — life, home, auto, long-term care. A life policy nobody knows about pays nobody. What matters here is the company name and policy number, not the fine print. *Lives:* one folder, one location, listed in Keepsake by company name only.
- **Titles** — ownership papers for vehicles, boats, trailers. Needed to sell or transfer; replaceable through the DMV if lost. *Lives:* with the deed is fine.
- *Supporting cast (one line each):* birth & marriage certificates, Social Security card, military discharge (DD-214 — it unlocks veteran burial benefits), and any prior divorce decree. Replaceable, but slow — knowing where they are is the win.

### Task list (one per session card)

1. **Find the spot.** ~15 min. Where do papers live today — even if it's three spots? Write the honest answer in your binder: *"Most papers: fireproof box, bedroom closet. Older things: green folder, desk."* No tidying required. **Done when:** the note exists.
2. **The six-paper check.** ~15 min. Go down the list — will, POA, advance directive, deed, insurance, titles — and mark each: **Have it / Don't have it / Not sure**. "Don't have it" is a normal answer, not a failing grade; most people find two or three gaps. **Done when:** all six are marked.
3. **Record the details.** ~15 min. For each "Have it": where it is, roughly when it was signed, and who else can get to it. (In words your family understands — never combinations or codes, just as everywhere in Keepsake.) For your will: who has the *original*. **Done when:** every "Have it" has a location.
4. **Plan the gaps.** ~15 min. For each missing paper, pick one next step from the honest menu: *call an attorney* (we explain what a first meeting costs and what to bring), *download your state's free advance-directive form*, or *"not yet — ask me again in three months"* — a real choice, recorded without judgment. **Done when:** every gap has a chosen next step, including "not yet."
5. **Tell one person.** ~10 min. Your trusted contact should know the papers *exist* and *where* — not what's in them. One tap shares the locations note; contents stay yours. **Done when:** shared (or deliberately deferred to Module 7).

### The completion moment

> **Your papers have a map. 🗂**
> Three weeks ago, the answer to "where are Mom's important papers?" was a search. Now it's a page. You checked six documents, recorded where five live, and made a plan for the one that's missing — on your schedule, by your choice.
>
> Here's the part worth sitting with: **most families never do this.** Yours just did.
>
> *Added to your binder:* "Where my important papers are" — updated · Document checklist — 5 of 6 · Next step you chose: call about a will, October.
> *When you're ready:* the next visit is a short one — your health wishes, two questions, entirely yours to answer. Or add another story first. Both count.

*(Design notes: the moment names concrete artifacts, credits the senior's agency twice, normalizes the gap, and offers a warm off-ramp — "add another story first" — honoring the never-two-hard-sessions rule. The status line uses only owner-attested facts, per the truth rule.)*

## Part 5 — Top 5 Curriculum Decisions (ordered)

1. **Stories before statutes — the warmth gradient is non-negotiable.** The senior's first three sessions must produce a captured story before any document is named. This is the single highest-leverage retention decision: it converts Keepsake's existing strength (the story layer) into the emotional trust that funds the 🕯4–5 modules, and it is the moat no legal-forms competitor can copy.

2. **Keepsake teaches and records; it never drafts or advises — stated inside every module.** Each module carries its own explicit "what needs a professional" boundary (attorney, state form, clinician), extending the app's truth rule into pedagogy. This is simultaneously the compliance posture and the trust posture: the curriculum's willingness to say "this part isn't us" is what makes seniors believe the parts that are.

3. **The 15-minute session with a named completion moment is the atomic unit.** One artifact or one decision per session, progress shown as rooms-of-your-life lit rather than percent complete, and a hard rule of never two 🕯4+ sessions consecutively. Preparedness curricula die of feeling infinite; this one is engineered to end, visibly, over and over.

4. **Two tracks that unlock each other: the family conversation is taught, not hoped for.** The adult child gets a parallel helper curriculum (help-without-taking-over, gap-routing, Module 8), enters at the trusted-contact moment framed as the senior's choice, and Module 7 hands both sides actual scripts and the fairness toolkit. This decision is where the curriculum earns the child-paid gift flow without ever making the senior feel managed.

5. **One curriculum, four doors — trigger-aware re-sequencing with the capacity-clock exception.** Downsizing, widowhood, diagnosis, and "the kids asked" reorder the same eight modules rather than spawning new content. The one place urgency is allowed to override warmth-first is the diagnosis path (documents require capacity; voice capture while speaking is easy), and the one place stories are deferred is early widowhood — both exceptions are themselves teaching decisions, documented so product never "optimizes" them away.
